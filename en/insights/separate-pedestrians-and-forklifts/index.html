How to separate pedestrians and forklifts in a warehouse: where and with what

To separate pedestrians and forklifts in a warehouse, start from the places where people and forklifts meet, not from a roll of tape. Collisions tend to happen at crossing points, rack corners, doorways, docks and on the routes people take as shortcuts. Each spot gets a kind of protection that fits its level of risk.

Laying out warehouse walkways: the short answer

Map the routes people really walk, map the routes forklifts really run, then deal with each spot where the two meet. Where people and forklifts run side by side, mark lines or put a barrier along the walkway. Where people cross a forklift route, put in a stop point or a gate, so everyone stops and looks before crossing.

Three levels of separating people from forklifts

There are three levels of separation between walkways and forklift routes. Each level is stronger than the one before.

  • Level 1, floor lines: quick and low cost. But a line only reminds. It holds no one back.
  • Level 2, a barrier along the walkway: people can no longer step into the forklift route. Where forklifts run close, the barrier takes the hit instead of a person.
  • Level 3, a gate at the crossing point: everyone has to stop, look both ways, then step across.
Do this now: The number of vehicles, their speed and the spots that get hit decide which level to stop at. See also three levels of separating people from forklifts.

Choosing the level of protection for each spot

Of the 1,021 workers who died in forklift-related incidents in the US from 1980 to 1994, 20% were workers on foot struck by a forklift, according to old NIOSH data. NIOSH recommends separating forklift traffic and workers on foot where possible, and limiting some aisles to workers on foot only or forklifts only.

The table below is a starting point for each spot. The exact level is set after looking at the real number of vehicles, their speed and the flow of people.

Start with a map of the real routes

Do not draw the walkway from the old floor plan. Stand in the warehouse at peak hours and watch where people walk from and to: from the office door to the rack area, from the toilets back to the workstation, from the gate into the picking area.

Walkway width and barrier setback from the lane edge are set from the vehicle type, load and floor plan during the survey. EU Directive 89/654/EEC, Annex I, point 12, gives no general figure either. It requires a sufficient safety clearance for pedestrians where vehicles use a route, and sufficient clearance between vehicle routes and doors, gates, pedestrian passages, corridors and staircases.

At the same time, mark the routes forklifts run and the places they turn. Lay the two maps over each other. Each crossing is a point to deal with. Each stretch where the two routes run close together is a stretch to separate. On the map, mark these four kinds of spots:

  • Where people step out: office doors, toilets, break rooms, dock doors.
  • Where forklifts change direction: corners, turning spots, rack ends.
  • Where the walkway is easily blocked: staging areas, empty pallets left temporarily.
  • Traces of the real routes: worn marks on the floor, impact marks on columns and rack uprights.

Crossing points and doorways

The crossing point is where people are most likely to be hit. A tall pallet load blocks most of the driver's view. Electric forklifts run quietly, and you hear them only when they are right next to you. People assume the driver has seen them. The areas a driver cannot see around the truck are covered in the article on forklift blind spots near walkways.

Office doors, toilet doors and break room doors that open straight onto a forklift route make crossings that are hard to predict, because people step out before they have had time to look. There, put a short barrier in front of the door so people have to turn into the walkway, or a self-closing gate so they stop before they step out. Put the stop line where a person is still inside the walkway but can see both directions of the forklift route.

HSE (UK) advises barriers at building entrances and exits, at building corners, and where people could walk straight onto a vehicle route. At crossing points, people on foot and drivers should be able to see clearly in all directions. NIOSH (US) recommends restricting forklifts near time clocks, break rooms, cafeterias and main exits, especially at shift ends and breaks, when the most people are on foot.

A convex mirror at a blind corner helps people and drivers see each other sooner. A mirror only helps you see. It holds no one back, so use it together with a stop line or a gate, not instead of them. NIOSH also suggests checking each intersection and blind corner to decide where a dome mirror would help.

Rack corners and rack ends

Forklifts turn at the end of the aisle. The forks or the pallet clip the rack upright. People coming out of a rack aisle step onto the main aisle just as a forklift takes the corner.

  • Rack ends facing the main aisle: a double-rail barrier or bollards, so forklifts do not hug the rack corner.
  • Rack uprights along the forklift lane: upright guards to reduce damage when they are clipped.
  • Where a rack aisle opens onto the main aisle: a stop line for pedestrians, with a mirror at the blind corner.
Do this now: Upright guards do not replace regular rack inspections. See also rack ends and rack uprights.

Dock area and dispatch

At dispatch time, forklifts come and go nonstop. Pallets waiting to ship spill into the walkway and push people into the forklift lane. A walkway that looks good on paper disappears if staged goods have no place of their own.

  • Mark out the staging area for outbound goods with lines, separate from the walkway.
  • Put a barrier along the walkway where it runs beside the dock doors.
  • Guard the dock edge where people and vehicles could back up to the floor edge.
Do this now: Dock areas often change how they stage goods by season, so survey at peak dispatch time. See also dock doors and dispatch area.

Where people take shortcuts

If the walkway is longer than the shortcut, people will take the shortcut. That is a layout fault. Awareness is only part of it. Telling people to be careful has never stopped a forklift. HSE (UK) also advises that pedestrian routes follow the paths people would naturally take, often called desire lines.

Look for traces of shortcuts: worn lines where there is no route, gaps between two barrier stretches, spaces between two rows of pallets. If the shortcut is reasonable, make it an official route with lines and a stop point. If the shortcut runs through a zone with dense forklift traffic, block it with a barrier and open another route that is closer.

Take a warehouse where the toilets are on the far side of the main forklift route. The walkway around is much longer, so people keep cutting across. More lines will not change that habit. The fix is to open a crossing point with a gate right where people already cross, and to put a barrier along the rest.

Do this now: stand five minutes at a crossing

Stand for five minutes at the busiest crossing point in the warehouse. Count how many people walk through without stopping to look. Take photos of that spot and of any places with signs of shortcuts. If the warehouse runs a night shift, stand there again at night, because the lighting and the flow of people differ from the day shift.

FAQ

Are floor lines alone enough for a warehouse walkway?

They are enough where traffic is light, forklifts are slow and people do not walk right beside vehicles. They are not enough where people and forklifts pass close together or traffic is dense, because a line only reminds. It holds no one back. There you need a barrier along the walkway.

Which crossing points should have a gate?

Where people step straight onto a forklift route before they have time to look: office doors, toilet doors, break room doors, where a rack aisle opens onto the main forklift route, and dock doors. A gate makes people stop before they cross.

Do we need a convex mirror at rack corners?

A mirror helps people and drivers see each other sooner at a blind corner. But a mirror only helps you see. It holds no one back. Use a mirror together with a stop line or a gate, not instead of them. Check each blind corner: a mirror is needed only where people and drivers cannot see each other.

How do we find where people take shortcuts?

Look for worn lines where there is no route, gaps between two barrier stretches and spaces between two rows of pallets. Watch during breaks and shift changes, when people move around the most. Ask the shift leaders too. They usually know where people cut across.

Do we have to shut the warehouse to change the walkway layout?

Usually not. Work zone by zone, starting with the most dangerous crossing point. Lines made with floor tape can be walked and driven over right after they are applied. Barriers are installed stretch by stretch so forklifts keep running. Each time a route changes, tell every shift before it takes effect.

How wide should a warehouse walkway be?

This article gives no general figure, because the width depends on how many people walk at once, whether they push hand carts or carry goods, and the clearance needed between the barrier and the edge of the forklift route. EU Directive 89/654/EEC also only requires a sufficient safety clearance, with no figure. We set the width during the survey, based on the real vehicles and layout.
