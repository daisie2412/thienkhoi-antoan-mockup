Forklift barriers: steel or polymer, chosen stretch by stretch

Don't replace every steel barrier with polymer. And don't keep steel where it gets hit every day. This article gives a rule for choosing steel or polymer forklift barriers for each stretch of your warehouse, plus a way to check it yourself in one walk-through.

Steel or polymer: the short answer

Choose for each stretch of barrier, not one type for the whole warehouse. Where traffic is light, vehicles are small and hits are rare, keep steel. Where hits are frequent, such as rack ends, corners and dock doors, switch to polymer. A hit dents steel and it has to be repaired. Polymer flexes with the hit and returns to its shape.

Steel barriers fail in the same way every time

Steel barriers are strong. But one forklift hit and the rail is dented. A dented rail does not straighten itself. You straighten it, weld it, or cut it out and fit a new one.

The force does not stop at the rail. It travels down to the base plate. The base pulls loose and the concrete around the bolts cracks. The paint chips off where it was hit, and soon after, it rusts.

A spot that gets hit will get hit again. Every hit means a repair, and every repair usually means closing that area. Signs that a stretch of steel barrier is failing repeatedly:

  • The rail has been straightened or touched up with paint several times in the same place
  • The base plate is shifted, the bolts are loose, or the concrete around the base has cracks
  • A dented stretch leans into the walkway and sits there waiting for a repair date

Polymer barriers take a hit differently

A polymer rail flexes to absorb the force, then stands back up. That is the biggest difference from steel. This holds only while the hit stays within the level the barrier is designed for. Heavier, faster vehicles need a larger barrier series, so the size is chosen by the vehicle type at each location.

Maintenance is different too. According to the maintenance guide in the manufacturer's catalogue, the routine work is cleaning and checking bolts and joints. There is no repainting step.

To see what the rail does when it is hit, watch the video of a forklift driving straight into a barrier. It is the manufacturer's test video.

The rule: choose stretch by stretch

Our rule: judge each stretch by how often it gets hit.

  • Aisles with light traffic, light vehicles and hand pallet trucks: keep steel. The upfront cost is lower and hits are rare.
  • Rack ends, corners, dock doors, anywhere traffic is dense and hits keep happening: switch to polymer.
  • Stretches where people walk right beside the forklift lane: give priority to a pedestrian barrier, because there the barrier takes the hit instead of a person.
  • Stretches still in good shape with no impact marks: no need to change yet. Put them on a watch list.
Do this now: You do not have to change the whole warehouse at once. Replace the worst stretches first and leave the rest for the next round.

A common case: rack ends and dock doors

Take a pallet warehouse with a main aisle running across the rack ends. Forklifts go in and out of each aisle and turn at the far end. The steel barrier near the dock doors is dented again and again, while the stretch at the back of the warehouse is almost untouched.

A sensible approach: change the rack ends near the dock to polymer first and leave the back of the warehouse as it is. On those aisles, add upright guards as well, because forks clip the uprights when forklifts turn. See also rack ends and rack uprights.

The dock door is the second spot. At dispatch time, forklifts come and go nonstop and pallets waiting to ship spill into the walkway. The barrier at the edge of the walkway beside the dock is worth changing. The barrier behind a row of machines, where few forklifts run, can stay steel. If the spot that gets hit is right next to where people walk, also read how to separate pedestrians from forklifts.

Do this now: Upright guards reduce damage when clipped. They do not replace regular rack inspections.

Installing and maintaining polymer barriers

A barrier holds only if its base is fixed properly into a floor that is thick enough. So we check the floor first, then decide the barrier size and how the base is fixed. The barrier series for each location are on the polymer barriers page.

Floor thickness and bolt type are confirmed during the survey, based on the barrier series and the actual floor in each area.

Maintenance schedule, following the guide in the manufacturer's catalogue:

  • Monthly: wipe with a soft, dry cloth. Do not use strong chemicals or abrasive materials. Frequency depends on the actual environment.
  • Every six months: check the base bolts, the joints and the end posts. At spots that get hit often, push and shake them by hand.
  • Yearly: check the tightening torque of expansion bolts with a torque wrench: 50 Nm for M10 bolts, 80 Nm for M12 bolts. If the base is fixed with chemical anchor, you can skip this check.
  • After every hit: check the point of impact, the base bolts and the joints between rail and post. Count the damaged parts and take photos.

Do this now: count the dented stretches

Walk the warehouse and count the steel barrier stretches that are dented, straightened or touched up with paint. Note where they are and roughly how many meters long. That is your list of stretches to change first.

FAQ

Should we replace all the steel barriers in the warehouse with polymer?

No. Where traffic is light, vehicles are small and hits are rare, steel still does the job at a lower upfront cost. Change only the stretches that are hit again and again, such as rack ends, corners and dock doors. Work in rounds, urgent stretches first.

Can a polymer barrier take every forklift hit?

No. Each barrier series takes a hit up to a certain level. Heavier, faster vehicles need a larger series and a stronger base. That is why the size is chosen by vehicle type and location, after measuring on site.

Does a polymer barrier need repainting like a steel one?

No. According to the manufacturer's maintenance guide, the routine work is cleaning, checking bolts and joints, and checking the tightening torque of expansion bolts. There is no repainting step.

What should we do after a hard hit on a barrier?

Check the point of impact, the base bolts and the joints between rail and post. Count the damaged parts and take photos. Repair or replace the damaged or loose part following the manufacturer's guide, then watch that stretch more closely.

Do we have to shut down the warehouse area to install polymer barriers?

Usually not. Work in rounds, urgent stretches first, and split the rest by area so the warehouse keeps running. The exact schedule is set during the site survey.
