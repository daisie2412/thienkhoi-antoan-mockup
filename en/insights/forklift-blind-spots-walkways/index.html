Forklift blind spots: why drivers miss pedestrians and where to stop collisions

Forklift blind spots are the areas around the truck that the driver cannot see from the seat, even after turning around and checking the mirrors. Pedestrians can see the truck, so they assume the driver sees them too. To stop collisions, first keep people and trucks from meeting where the view is blocked.

What are forklift blind spots, and where are they?

You see the truck. The truck does not see you. Forklift blind spots are the areas the driver cannot see: ahead when the mast and load block the view, behind when reversing, and the diagonals behind the overhead guard posts. In a warehouse they meet pedestrians at rack ends, intersections, doorways and the dock.

Why do forklifts have blind spots?

The truck's own structure cuts off part of the driver's view. Racking, doors, noise and darkness take the rest. OSHA notes that blocked visibility, even partly blocked, increases the chance of accidents.

  • Mast: the uprights, lift cylinder and chains sit right in front of the driver. A person standing behind one upright can disappear completely.
  • Load: once the load is higher than the driver's eyes, the view ahead is effectively closed off.
  • Reversing: the driver looks back over one shoulder, so the other side and the area right behind the counterweight are almost invisible.
  • Rack ends: the fork tips poke into the main aisle while the driver is still behind the end of the rack and cannot see who is coming.
  • Doorways: people step out of offices and toilets without looking at the truck route. At the main door, strip curtains and the change in light blur anyone behind them.
  • Intersections: racking, columns or staged goods hide the corner. When the truck turns, its tail swings out to the side opposite the turn.
  • Noise: compressors and extraction fans drown out the truck. Electric forklifts make almost no engine noise, and a reversing alarm that beeps all shift turns into background noise.
  • Night shift: a dead lamp at a rack corner, a dark dock, people in dark clothes, and everyone tired by the end of the shift.

Where in a warehouse do forklifts hit pedestrians?

A typical case: a truck carrying two layers of cartons reverses out of a rack aisle, the driver looking back over the right shoulder. A picker steps out of the aisle on the left, scanner in hand, earplugs in. Neither knows the other is there.

In what order should you block collisions?

For pedestrian safety in a warehouse, the measures that rely least on memory and attention come first. OSHA (international reference) advises separating pedestrians from forklifts with walkways, railings or barriers, and using floor striping only where barriers cannot be used. The UK HSE calls separating people from vehicle activity the most effective way. The order:

  • Barriers between the walkway and the truck route.
  • Gates at crossing points.
  • Floor lines for walkways, stop lines and no-standing zones.
  • Convex mirrors and warning lights.
  • Speed and reversing rules.
  • The horn at blind corners, doorways and rack ends.

Where do you need barriers that fully separate people and trucks?

A barrier keeps people inside the walkway, even when they are carrying a box or looking at a phone, and it stands between them and the truck when a driver cuts a corner too tight.

Start with walkway sections that run alongside a truck route and the places where people step straight out. The UK HSE recommends barriers or guard rails at building entrances and exits, at building corners, and where people could walk straight onto a vehicle route. Thien Khoi fixes the barrier's setback from the edge of the truck route during the site survey. See the layout on the walkways and crossing points page.

What do you do where people must cross the truck route?

From the office to the racking, from the toilets back to the workstation, people have to cross the truck route. There, leave a gap in the barrier and fit a self-closing gate. The gate makes people pause for a moment to look both ways, then closes behind them.

Place the gate where a person standing behind it can see both directions of the truck route. The UK HSE advises that crossing points be clearly marked and signposted. The guide to separating walkways from forklift routes covers each type of crossing, and gate models are in the barrier and self-closing gate range.

How far do floor lines, mirrors and warning lights go?

Floor lines show whose lane is whose and where to stop. On quiet stretches where trucks move slowly, lines may be enough. Where traffic is heavy, they are only a reminder. Walkway marking tape can be walked and driven over right after it is applied.

Convex mirrors and warning lights help people and drivers see each other sooner at blind corners. A dusty or knocked mirror stops working, and a person with their back turned does not see the light, so both are an extra layer after barriers and gates.

Do this now: Clean and readjust the convex mirrors at blind corners during this shift.

What should speed, horn and reversing rules say?

Driving rules are the last layer because they depend on drivers getting it right every time. OSHA's forklift standard (international reference) requires drivers to slow down and sound the horn at cross aisles and wherever the view is obstructed, and to travel with the load trailing when it blocks the forward view.

Reversing brings its own risk: the UK HSE reports that nearly a quarter of all deaths involving workplace transport occur during reversing, and advises organising routes to reduce the need to reverse. When you write your site rules, check the current Vietnamese occupational safety and hygiene regulations.

  • A maximum speed for each zone, posted on signs, lower where people work.
  • Rack ends, doorways, intersections: slow down, sound the horn, look, then go.
  • Where a blind spot cannot be avoided, use a spotter.
  • No overtaking at intersections or blind spots.
  • At marked crossings, trucks give way to pedestrians.
Do this now: Post these rules where drivers collect their trucks and read them out at the start-of-shift briefing.

What can a warehouse manager do this week?

You do not need to buy anything to start. One task a day, and by the end of the week you will have a blind spot map of your own warehouse. To check other hazards on the same walk, use the 10-point checklist.

  • Day 1: park a truck with the power off and forks lowered at the rack end nearest the door. Sit in the driver's seat and have someone walk slowly around it. Wherever you lose sight of them, ask them to chalk a mark on the floor.
  • Day 2: stand at the busiest intersection for ten minutes at peak time and count the trucks that pass without sounding the horn.
  • Day 3: walk the warehouse on the night shift and note dead lamps, dark corners and faded lines.
  • Day 4: ask drivers where they often brake hard and where they have nearly hit someone.
  • Day 5: check the mirrors, horn, reversing alarm and warning lights on every truck.
  • Day 6: put everything on the warehouse map and rank it by the order of controls above.

FAQ

Does a convex mirror get rid of forklift blind spots?

No. A mirror helps drivers and pedestrians see each other sooner, and OSHA advises convex mirrors at blind aisle intersections. But the image is small and distorted, dust blurs it and a knock throws it out of line. A mirror holds no one back, so pair it with a stop line or a gate.

Why do forklifts drive in reverse with bulky loads?

Because the load blocks the view ahead. OSHA (international reference) requires drivers to travel with the load trailing when it obstructs the forward view. Reversing shows the path, but it hides the side the driver is not looking over and the area right behind the counterweight, so reversing routes must be kept clear of people.

How far should pedestrians stay from a forklift?

We have not found a figure with a clear source that applies to every warehouse, so we do not give one here. Rather than telling people to keep their distance, lay the site out so they never have to stand near trucks: walkway barriers, a separate standing area during loading, gates where people cross.

Are warning lights on a forklift enough to protect pedestrians?

Not on their own. A light spot or line projected on the floor warns that a truck is coming, which helps at blind corners. The UK HSE suggests flashing warning lights where a reversing alarm might not stand out from background noise. But someone with their back turned or bent over their work will not see the light.

Can cameras and anti-collision sensors replace barriers?

No. The UK HSE notes that sensing systems can warn the driver or stop the vehicle when something is detected close behind it, but also says no single measure is likely to be enough to ensure safety. A barrier stays in place when the electronics fail.
