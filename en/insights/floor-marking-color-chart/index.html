Floor marking colors: common practice or regulation

Floor marking colors mostly follow 5S practice and each plant's own rules. We have not found a general regulation that requires every plant to use the same floor marking color chart. So each plant needs to set one chart, post it at the entrance and use it consistently.

Do this now: Safety signs, emergency lights and exit signs, and fire protection equipment have their own rules. Companies should check the current regulations on occupational safety and fire protection. A floor marking color chart does not replace those rules.

Why each plant needs its own color chart

A floor line only helps when everyone reads it the same way. One plant uses yellow for walkways, the plant next door uses yellow for forklift routes. Staff changing shifts, temporary workers and contractor drivers who pass through both will misread it.

A more common problem is inside a single plant. Each round of marking is done by a different person, and each purchase brings a slightly different tape color. Over time, one color carries three meanings in three zones.

A written color chart solves this. It is also the document for training new people and for 5S audits.

Setting a plant color chart, step by step

  • Walk the plant and photograph every type of line in use. Note the color and the meaning it carries now.
  • Gather them into one chart, one main meaning per color. If a color carries two meanings, separate them.
  • Add the line style (solid, dashed, striped) and the tape width for each type of line.
  • Print the chart and post it at the factory entrance and in the training room. The next round of marking follows the chart, not whichever roll of tape is left in the store.
  • Tape width follows the type of line: walkway and lane lines are usually wider than pallet-bay lines; Thien Khoi suggests exact widths during the survey.

A case: one yellow, two meanings

Suppose a plant opens a new warehouse area. The old area uses yellow for walkways. The new area was done by a different team, with yellow for forklift routes and white for walkways. A person walking from the old area to the new one follows the yellow line, and steps into the forklift route.

The fix is not hard: set one meaning for yellow, redo the wrong stretch, update the chart at the entrance. The hard part is noticing the problem. So each time a new area opens, the person in charge of 5S should check the color chart before the lines are marked.

Color and tape type: decide together

The color is the meaning, and the tape type decides how long the line lasts in that zone. PVC marking tape suits walkways and rack areas. Poorly lit spots need reflective tape or photoluminescent tape. Steps, ramps and slippery spots use anti-slip tape with colored stripes.

If you are still deciding between paint and tape for each zone, read the article floor marking with paint or tape. For separating people and vehicles, see also the article separating pedestrians and forklifts.

Do this now: Photoluminescent tape does not replace the emergency lighting or exit signs required by regulation. It is the last backup layer.

FAQ

Is there a mandatory color code for factory floor markings?

We have not found a general regulation that requires every plant to use the same floor marking color chart. Line colors mostly follow 5S practice and each plant's own rules. Safety signs, fire protection equipment and exit routes have their own rules, and companies should check the current regulations.

Should walkways be marked yellow or another color?

Yellow is the common color for aisles and lanes. Some plants use blue or green. Any color works as long as the whole plant uses one meaning and writes it in the color chart posted at the entrance.

Where do we use yellow and black lines?

At spots with a risk of impact or tripping: dock edges, step edges, protruding machine bases, columns at corners. Use them only where there is a real hazard. If they are used everywhere, people get used to them and stop noticing.

How do we change line colors to a new chart?

Work zone by zone, starting with the zones where people mix things up most. Painted lines have to be removed or painted over. Tape lines are peeled off and replaced. Choose the method for each zone using the article on paint or tape.

Where should the color chart be posted?

At the factory entrance, where everyone passes, and in the training room for new staff. When the layout changes or a new area opens, update the chart first and mark the lines after.

How often should we review the floor marking color chart?

There is no general interval. Review it when a new area opens, when the layout changes, when the people or team doing the marking change, or when a 5S audit finds one color carrying two meanings. Each time, update the posted copy and write the new effective date.
