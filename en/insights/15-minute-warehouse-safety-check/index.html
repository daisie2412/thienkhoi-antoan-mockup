Warehouse safety checklist: a 15-minute walk, 10 points to check

A warehouse safety checklist only helps if someone walks it every week, and that takes 15 minutes and a phone. Walk a fixed route while forklifts are running and photograph anything wrong at the 10 points. Next week, compare the new photos with the old ones to see what has been fixed.

A 15-minute warehouse safety check: how it works

Pick the hour when forklifts are busiest. Walk one loop along the flow of goods: dock doors, main aisle, rack ends, places where people cross, stairs, forklift parking. At each stop, check the points that belong there, photograph anything wrong, and note who fixes it and by when. Next week, walk the same route.

When to walk the warehouse and which route to take

Walk when the warehouse is busiest: afternoon dispatch or around shift change. In an empty warehouse every aisle is clear, and you will see nothing.

Once a month, add a night-shift walk and switch off the lights on the stairs and mezzanine for 30 seconds to check point 9.

Follow the flow of goods from the dock inwards, and walk the same route every time:

  • Minutes 0 to 3, dock doors and the outbound staging area: points 3, 4.
  • Minutes 3 to 6, main aisle and corners: points 1, 6.
  • Minutes 6 to 9, rack ends, uprights, steel barriers: points 5, 7.
  • Minutes 9 to 12, office and restroom doors, places where people cross: points 2, 4.
  • Minutes 12 to 15, stairs, ramps, forklift parking and chargers: points 8, 9, 10.
Do this now: Open the 10-point checklist on the website on your phone and tick as you go.

The 10 points: what good looks like and when to act at once

The ten points below follow the same order as the checklist on the website. If you see a sign from the last column, report it and block it off during that shift.

Points 1 to 4: walkways, crossings, goods in the walkway

Lines fade first on corners and in front of doors, where turning wheels scrub the floor.

Point 3 shows most clearly at the dock in the afternoon: a truck arrives late, outgoing pallets creep into the walkway, and people walk around them through the forklift lane.

Points 2 and 4 share one danger: people step out before they look. How to block each spot is covered in separating walkways from forklift routes; before you re-mark lines, settle the colors with a floor marking color chart.

International reference: OSHA (United States) requires aisles to be kept clear and permanent aisles to be marked; the HSE workplace transport checklist (UK) asks whether vehicles and pedestrians are kept safely apart.

Points 5 to 7: rack uprights, columns and steel barriers that get hit

Fresh marks show bright metal and paint flakes still on the floor; old marks are dull and dusty. Three weeks of fresh marks in the same place means a forklift passes that close every day: protect that spot before a hard hit.

If an upright is bent, tape off the bay, stop loading it and tell whoever looks after the racking at once. What to check after each hit is in forklift hit the rack: what to check.

For dented steel barriers, count the sections and note where they are. Once steel has been dented and straightened, it is hard to know whether it will hold the next hit.

Points 8 to 10: wet floors, step edges in the dark, forklift parking

Floors get wet in a few familiar places: the foot of a ramp when rain blows in, wash areas, roof leaks, cold store doors. Write those places down. For step edges, shine a torch along the stairs; where the edge reflects, it is worn.

If you cannot see the step edges or the way out with the lights off, fix it before a real power cut.

Marks on the wall behind the parking bays show how far forklifts back up. A hit on a charger needs action at once, because there is power inside. The HSE checklist also asks whether there is enough safe parking and whether lighting in each area is sufficient.

How to record it and what to fix first

Write one line for each problem: point number, location, photo, who fixes it, deadline. Take photos from the same spot and name them by date and point number.

OSHA advises documenting inspections so you can later verify that hazards were corrected, and dealing with the greatest risks first. In a warehouse, that means anywhere a person and a forklift can touch:

  • During the shift: points 2, 3, 4 when people have to step into a forklift lane; point 5 when an upright is bent; point 10 when a charger has been hit.
  • Within the week: points 6, 7 when impact marks repeat; point 8 when there is standing water.
  • Within the month: point 1 when lines are fading; point 8 when step edges start to wear; point 9 when you can still just see in the dark.
Do this now: Get the printable 10-point warehouse survey checklist and pin it at the shift lead's desk.

Repeat every week: who should do it

Give it to one named person, usually the warehouse manager or safety lead, who walks at a fixed time each week on the same route. Take a forklift driver or team leader along, because they know where the near misses happen. OSHA also advises having workers take part in inspections.

Walk again straight away when the layout changes, forklifts or racking are added, or after a near miss. HSE says controls must be reviewed after changes like these and after any accident or near miss.

In Vietnam, Article 9 of Circular 07/2016/TT-BLĐTBXH requires employers to organize self-inspections of occupational safety and health, with the content and timing decided by the company. A full inspection is required at least once a year at company level and every 6 months at workshop or team level, or every 6 and 3 months in the high-risk industries listed in Article 8. The 15-minute walk sits between those inspections and does not replace them. Check the current text before writing it into your procedures.

When to bring in an outside survey

The checklist on the website grades the result by how many points you tick; use the same thresholds. Bent or cracked racking is a structural matter, so ask the racking supplier or a rack inspector to assess it.

  • 1 to 2 points: fix them within the week.
  • 3 to 5 points: book a survey soon, because problems pull each other along, as when goods in the walkway push people to cut across forklift lanes.
  • 6 points or more: the warehouse has many danger spots, and the routes for people and forklifts need redrawing.
  • One point ticked three weeks in a row despite fixes: the fix is not working.
  • Before you change the layout, add forklifts or add racking.

FAQ

How often should you run a warehouse safety check?

Do the 15-minute walk every week at a fixed time, and walk again at once when the layout changes or after a near miss. Under Vietnam's Circular 07/2016/TT-BLĐTBXH, full inspections are required at least once a year at company level and every 6 months at workshop level, more often in high-risk industries.

What should a warehouse safety checklist include?

Put first the places where people and forklifts meet: walkway lines, crossings, goods in walkways, doors opening onto lanes. Add the places forklifts hit structures, such as rack uprights, columns, barriers and parking, then wet floors and step edges with the lights off.

Do we need to record a warehouse self-check?

Yes. Without a record, nobody knows next week what has been fixed. For the weekly walk, a five-column table is enough: point number, location, photo, who fixes it, deadline. Periodic full inspections follow the self-inspection procedure your company has issued.

How many ticked points mean we need an outside survey?

Using the grading on the website checklist: 1 to 2 points, fix them yourself within the week; 3 to 5 points, book a survey soon; 6 or more, the warehouse has many danger spots. One point ticked three weeks in a row despite fixes also needs someone on site.

Does a 15-minute walk replace a risk assessment?

No. The walk only catches what you can see during a shift: impact marks, faded lines, goods in walkways. A risk assessment also covers working methods, training, vehicles and procedures. HSE breaks it into five steps; the weekly walk helps most with identifying hazards and reviewing controls.
