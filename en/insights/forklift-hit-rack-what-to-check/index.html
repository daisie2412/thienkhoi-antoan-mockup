Forklift hit a rack: what to check first and who decides it can stay in use

If a forklift hit a rack, what to check first is the bays around the impact: stop all loading there and move people out of that aisle. Reporting, photos and a written record come next, then a look at each part and a sign-off from someone qualified. This article follows that order, with a table of warning signs and ways to protect rack uprights next time.

Forklift hit a rack: what do you do first?

Stop loading the bays that rest on the damaged upright, cordon off the area, report to the racking safety lead, photograph and record. Then look at the upright, base plate, floor anchors, bracing and beam locks. Do not load, straighten or repair anything until the rack supplier or an inspection body says the rack can stay in use.

Five things to do in the first hour

If anyone is hurt, look after the person first. Print these five steps and put them up by the forklift chargers.

  • Stop loading: no putting away or picking in the bays on either side of the damaged upright, on every level, because the upright carries the beams of those bays. Taking goods off to reduce the load comes later, decided by the person in charge of the racking.
  • Cordon off: close the aisle in front of those bays with tape or temporary barriers and hang a no-loading sign. If the run leans or an upright has buckled, close the aisles on both sides of the run.
  • Report: tell the person responsible for racking safety or the shift lead straight away, even after a light scrape. If no one holds that role, name one person for each shift.
  • Photos: the whole bay, the impact point close up, the base plate and floor anchors, with the run and bay numbers. If the run leans, take the photos from a distance.
  • Record: the time, which truck, who was driving, whether the forks, the pallet or the truck body made contact, and which bays are closed. Record impacts that left no visible damage too.
Do this now: Put dated labels on the closed bays so the next shift knows which ones must not be loaded again.

Checking a rack after an impact: where do you look?

HSE in the UK says any damage reduces the load a rack can carry, and the more damage, the weaker it gets. A rack that still looks straight is no reason to load it again.

Most impacts land low, and corner uprights are especially at risk. Stand on the floor and work up from the base plate in the order of the table.

How is rack damage graded?

International practice grades rack damage in three levels: green, amber and red. The grading appears in HSG76 from HSE, the UK workplace safety regulator, and in guidance from SEMA, the UK storage equipment manufacturers' association. The European standard EN 15635 sets rules for measuring and classifying damage to uprights and bracing. This is international reference, not Vietnamese regulation.

A level is set by measuring, done by a technically competent person. This article does not copy measurement limits, because a figure taken from a different rack system is more dangerous than no figure.

  • Red, very serious damage: offload at once and isolate the area until it is repaired, usually by replacing the damaged part.
  • Amber, hazardous damage: repair soon. Once offloaded, do not reload until repaired. If still not repaired after four weeks, it becomes red.
  • Green: within limits, no repair yet, but record it and look closely at the next inspection. SEMA says green does not mean the damage is acceptable.
Do this now: Ask your rack supplier for the measurement limits for your rack system and keep them in the rack log.

Who decides the rack can stay in use?

Site staff carry out the five steps and close the bays. The people who say a rack can carry load again are the rack supplier, meaning the manufacturer or the installer, or an inspection body with racking expertise. HSG76 also counts a trained racking specialist in your own company. If you have no such person, call the rack supplier.

Thien Khoi supplies barriers and floor tape. We do not assess rack structures and we do not declare a rack safe. We look at where trucks hit and recommend how to keep them away. Until someone qualified has signed off, do not:

  • Straighten an upright with a jack, a hammer or the forks. SEMA does not recommend straightening damaged uprights with hydraulic rams.
  • Weld anything onto the rack. HSE says racking should never be altered, for example by welding, without first consulting the manufacturer.
  • Swap in parts from a different system. SEMA advises parts approved by the rack manufacturer, fitted by trained racking installers.
  • Move beams to dodge the damage. Moving beams, especially the two lowest, needs a fresh check of the rack's load capacity.
  • Reload a bay because the rack still looks straight.

Where and when do forklifts hit racks?

At the end of a run next to the main aisle, the truck turns as it leaves the run, the counterweight swings out, or a pallet corner sweeps the end upright. HSE also notes that corner uprights are especially at risk.

At a junction between forklift aisles, two trucks meet, one reverses to give way and touches the rack behind it, where the driver cannot see.

Next to a dock door at dispatch time, pallets left at the end of a run narrow the aisle, and hurried drivers take the corner close to the upright.

On the night shift, nobody sees the impact. Next morning the day shift finds fresh paint on an upright, and the bay has carried goods all night. An impact nobody reports is the most dangerous one, and if drivers who report a hit get punished, nobody will report the next one.

Protecting rack uprights: guards where trucks actually hit

HSG76 advises protecting racking wherever lift trucks may strike it, using renewable column guards, and fitting corner uprights with protection in a conspicuous colour. OSHA, the US workplace safety agency, also advises rack upright guards. See our rack guards and forklift barriers.

  • Start with end-of-run uprights, corner uprights, uprights along main forklift aisles and near dock doors.
  • A guard takes the knock in place of the upright and reduces damage. It adds no load capacity and does not replace rack inspection.
  • If a truck hits the guard, still check the upright behind it.
  • Measure the aisle before fitting. HSE warns that adding upright guards to an aisle that never had them can reduce the clearance trucks need and sometimes increase damage.

How do you keep trucks away from rack uprights?

A guard only takes the hit once a truck has made contact. To make contact less likely in the first place, add these three measures.

  • End barriers and floor rails: a low barrier in front of the end upright so the counterweight and forks hit the barrier first, and a floor rail along the rack where trucks run close. See the layout on rack ends and rack uprights.
  • Wide, clear aisles: HSE says aisles should be wide enough for trucks to manoeuvre easily, depending on the type of truck. Keep pallets off the ends of runs. Mark forklift aisle boundaries with floor marking tape, as OSHA advises.
  • Driver training: HSE notes that driver skill has a great bearing on how much damage racking takes. Practise turning at the end of a run, lower pallets onto beams without sliding them, and report every contact with the rack at once.
Do this now: After every hard hit, check the barrier, its base and the rack upright behind it, even if the barrier has sprung back into shape.

Do this now: walk the ends of your rack runs

During this shift, walk along the ends of the rack runs. Photograph every dent and fresh paint scrape on the uprights and log the location in the rack log. Any mark missing from the log is an impact nobody reported.

FAQ

A forklift only scraped the rack and it still looks straight. Do we need to report it?

Yes. HSE's HSG76 says any damage reduces the load a rack can carry, and anyone who sees damage should report it at once to the person responsible for racking safety. Photograph it, log it, then let that person decide whether to close bays and call in an inspector.

Can a dented upright be straightened or welded?

Not by you. SEMA does not recommend straightening damaged uprights with hydraulic rams, nor welding to straighten bent steel. HSE says racking should never be altered, for example by welding, without first consulting the manufacturer. A badly damaged upright is usually replaced with the correct part.

How often should racking be inspected?

Under HSG76, the person responsible for racking safety arranges visual checks weekly or at other intervals based on risk assessment. A technically competent person inspects at intervals of not more than 12 months. Every forklift impact adds one more check. Check current local regulations and your rack supplier's instructions too.

How long can an amber bay stay as it is?

Under HSG76 and SEMA guidance, an amber bay that has been offloaded must not be reloaded until it is repaired. If it is still not repaired after four weeks, it becomes red and must be isolated at once. This is international practice, given here for reference.

Who signs off a rack for use after an impact?

The rack supplier, meaning the manufacturer or the installer, or an inspection body with racking expertise. They inspect, measure and give a written verdict. Thien Khoi does not assess rack structures. We only recommend how to keep trucks away from the spots they hit.
